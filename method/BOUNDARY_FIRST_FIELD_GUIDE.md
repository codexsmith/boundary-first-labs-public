# Boundary First — A Small Field Guide

**Genre:** introductory method guide; not a novel theorem or professional substitute.

A system description is useful only relative to a question and the operations that follow. The method begins before a tool, model, dataset, or local optimization is selected.

## One pass over a consequential system

| Question | What to record |
|---|---|
| **Purpose** | The exact task and the people or processes affected |
| **State** | What is represented and how it is measured |
| **Boundary** | What is inside the model and what can cross its interfaces |
| **Admissibility** | Which transitions are defined, permitted and authorized |
| **Invariant** | What must survive the operation |
| **Observation** | What witness or evidence can test the result |
| **Loss** | What the representation combines or leaves unrecorded |
| **Consequence** | What happens outside the local calculation |
| **Repair** | Who can detect, contest, correct and reverify the result |

Use the minimum level of apparatus needed for the problem. A domain's established theory, standards, testing tools, and expert practices remain authoritative where they already solve it.

## A worked mathematical example

One number, area, correctly describes the size of a planar region but does not in general predict how the area changes when the region is expanded. The difference becomes clear for a disk and an equal-area square.

The full mathematics, standard sources, counterexample and limitations belong to one [public n-ball construction package](../projects/n-ball-boundary-recursion/README.md). This field guide **links** to that owner instead of duplicating its derivation.

## When not to generalize

A successful mathematical analogy is not evidence that a healthcare decision, financial transaction, physics model, or institutional rule has the same validity conditions. At a crossing between domains, declare the source and target assumptions, the missing information, the evidence required by the target, and any independent approval.

Where an established method is already sufficient, say so. Where a defect remains, preserve it. Where no verified consequence can be measured, don't invent one.

**Trace what follows. Design for repair.**
